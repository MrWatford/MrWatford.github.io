🔭 About this project

For this project, you will choose one planet in our solar system, research its characteristics, and create a colorful, organized poster board. Your poster should combine accurate science information with a large planet illustration or model. Someone looking at your finished board should be able to learn what makes your planet special.

  • 🙋Individual projectYou will research, write, and design your poster on your own.
  • 🪐One planet onlyYou are researching one planet, not building a model of the whole solar system.
  • 📚Research, not an experimentYou do not need a hypothesis, an experiment, or a scientific-method report.
  • ⭐Quality over costAccurate information, clear explanations, and thoughtful organization matter more than expensive supplies.

Due date: To be announced by Mr. Watford.

🔎 Research guide: Planet Facts

Find each item below and report it in your Planet Facts section. Keep this section short and easy to read. Labeled bullets or small fact cards work better than long paragraphs. Write down which source each fact came from.

A Position from the Sun

What number planet is it?

Report it like this“_____ is the _____ planet from the Sun.”

B Planet type

Is it a rocky (terrestrial) planet, a gas giant, or an ice giant?

Rocky planets have solid rocky surfaces. Gas giants and ice giants have deep atmospheres and do not have an Earth-like solid surface where a person could stand.

  • Rocky: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
  • Gas giants: Jupiter, Saturn
  • Ice giants: Uranus, Neptune

C Size

Find the planet’s approximate diameter. Diameter is the distance across a planet through its center.

Units required. Kilometers (km) are best; you may add miles too.

D Average distance from the Sun

Report an approximate average distance. A planet’s distance from the Sun changes during its orbit, so use its average distance.

Units required, such as km or miles.

E Rotation period: One complete spin

How long does the planet take to spin once?

Careful! One spin is not always the same as the time from one sunrise to the next. For example, NASA’s fact sheet lists Mercury’s rotation period as about 59 Earth days, but its time from sunrise to sunrise as about 176 Earth days. Use a source’s clearly labeled rotation period and don’t mix the two.

F Year length: One orbit around the Sun

How long does the planet take to travel around the Sun once?

Use Earth days or Earth years, and label which one.

G Temperature

Describe the temperature using a reliable source. Include units (°C or °F) and say what the number measures, such as:

  • Average surface temperature
  • Daytime and nighttime surface temperatures
  • Temperature at a certain level of the atmosphere or clouds

Gas giants and ice giants have no solid surface, so don’t call a cloud-top temperature a “surface” temperature.

H Atmosphere

Does it have a thick layer of gases around it? What is it mainly made of?

Mercury is special: it has only an extremely thin layer of gases called an exosphere, not an atmosphere like Earth’s.

I Surface or visible features

Describe its rocky surface, or the clouds and features we can see. Ideas: craters, volcanoes, deserts, oceans, clouds, storms, color bands.

Some planets are covered in clouds. Make it clear whether you are describing the clouds we see or the surface hidden underneath.

J Moons and rings

Does the planet have moons? Does it have rings?

  • Use a current, reliable source for the number of moons.
  • Write the date you checked, like “as of ____.” Counts can change as scientists confirm new discoveries.
  • If it has moons, name up to two examples.

Saturn’s rings are the easiest to see, but Saturn is not the only planet with rings. Check your planet!

K Exploration

Name at least one spacecraft or mission that studied your planet and explain what it did. For Earth, an Earth-observing satellite or mission works.

Flyby
Passes by the planet and studies it on the way.
Orbiter
Circles the planet to study it over time.
Lander
Touches down on the surface and stays in one place.
Rover
Lands, then drives around to explore.

Not every planet has been landed on. Gas giants and ice giants have no solid surface to land on.

L Comparison with Earth

Give at least one clear comparison with Earth:

  • Larger or smaller
  • Hotter or colder (describe which temperatures you compared)
  • Longer or shorter year
  • Different atmosphere or surface conditions

Chose Earth? Compare Earth with one other planet.

✍️ Three-paragraph research summary

Write three short paragraphs of about 3–5 complete sentences each. Your paragraphs should help readers understand your planet, not just repeat a list of numbers.

About the sentence starters: They are supports to help you get going, not a finished report to copy. Change the wording and make it sound like you!

Paragraph 1

Introduce your planet

  • Planet name
  • Position from the Sun
  • Planet type
  • Why you chose it

Sentence starter“The planet I researched is _____. It is the _____ planet from the Sun. It is classified as _____. I chose this planet because _____.”

Paragraph 2

Describe its environment

  • What it looks like
  • Its surface or atmosphere
  • Temperature or weather
  • One important feature

Sentence starter“One of the most noticeable features of _____ is _____. Its surface/atmosphere _____. These conditions make it different from Earth because _____.”

Paragraph 3

Explain what makes it interesting

  • Exploration
  • A surprising discovery or characteristic
  • What you learned

Sentence starter“Scientists have studied _____ using _____. One fact that surprised me was _____. The most interesting thing I learned was _____.”

💡 Did You Know?

Add a separate section on your board titled Did You Know? with three accurate facts that go beyond your Planet Facts chart. Questions you could investigate:

  • What unusual feature makes this planet recognizable?
  • Does it have a famous storm, volcano, canyon, or unusual tilt?
  • What is unusual about its rings or moons?
  • How did it get its name?
  • What discovery did a spacecraft make there?

Mr. WatfordChoose facts you understand well enough to explain in your own words. Avoid repeating the same fact in all three spaces.

🧭 Step-by-step directions

  1. Choose and get approval. Select one of the eight planets and confirm your choice with Mr. Watford.
  2. Gather reliable sources. Use at least two reliable sources, such as NASA, NASA Space Place, ESA, your school textbook, or an age-appropriate encyclopedia. Search results help you find a source. Open the actual page and read it before using its information.
  3. Take notes. Use the research organizer to collect facts. Write brief notes and record the source for each group of facts.
  4. Write your paragraphs. Write three short paragraphs in your own words. Check that you understand every sentence.
  5. Sketch your layout. Plan where your title, planet artwork, fact cards, paragraphs, diagram, and sources will go before gluing anything down.
  6. Create your planet. Draw, paint, or build a lightweight planet model. Include recognizable features and colors based on reliable reference images.
  7. Prepare your information. Write or type your text neatly. Use headings, readable lettering, and strong contrast between the text and the background.
  8. Arrange everything. Place all pieces on the board without attaching them. Check spacing and make sure nothing overlaps or hides important information.
  9. Attach and finish. Secure the pieces. Add simple decorations that support the space theme.
  10. Check your work. Use the final checklist, proofread, confirm your facts, and put your name and class on the back.

🎨 Design Your Planet Poster

Great planet posters from past classes had a few things in common:

  • Large, bold planet names
  • A big planet drawing or raised model
  • Black, navy, or other space-themed backgrounds
  • Research text mounted on contrasting paper
  • Neatly separated sections
  • Optional stars, borders, rings, and small pictures

A space background is optional. Any design works if it is easy to read.

🔠 Title

  • Put the planet’s name across the top or big along one side.
  • Make it readable from several feet away.
  • An optional subtitle adds interest, like “Mars: The Red Planet.”

🪐 Main planet artwork

  • Make it a main focal point, about 8–12 inches across when practical.
  • Leave enough room for readable information.
  • Keep features sensible. Add a caption if you show a cutaway, an exaggerated feature, or false colors.

🗂️ Research sections

  • Use headings like “Planet Facts,” “About My Planet,” “Did You Know?” and “Sources.”
  • On a dark background, mount text on light-colored cards.
  • Avoid tiny text or one crowded wall of writing.

☀️ Solar-system order strip

  • Show the Sun and all eight planets in the correct order.
  • Highlight or circle your planet.
  • Label it “Order only—not to scale.”
  • Keep it small so your planet stays the star of the show.

✨ Decorations

  • Optional stars, borders, or small spacecraft drawings.
  • Decorations should never cover text or replace research.

🖌️ Planet artwork ideas

Your main planet artwork should be your own work. Base your colors and features on reliable reference images. Open the idea for your planet:

Mercury

Gray rocky appearance with visible craters.

Venus

Pale cream or yellowish cloud-covered appearance. If you also show the hidden surface under the clouds, put it in a separate, labeled drawing.

Earth

Blue oceans, land, and white clouds.

Mars

Rust-red surface, darker regions, and polar ice.

Jupiter

Broad cloud bands and the Great Red Spot.

Saturn

Pale cloud bands and prominent rings.

Uranus

Pale blue-green appearance. An optional small diagram can show its unusual tilt.

Neptune

Blue appearance with subtle details in its atmosphere.

📸 About space photos

Some space photos have their colors enhanced to show details more clearly. Read the caption on your reference image and follow what it says about the colors.

Possible materials

  • Colored pencils or crayons
  • Markers
  • Paint
  • Construction paper
  • Layered cardboard
  • A paper plate
  • A lightweight pre-cut foam half-sphere
  • Small printed supporting images (with a source credit)
