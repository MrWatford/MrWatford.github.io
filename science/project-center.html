🔬 The Scientific Method: A Step-by-Step Guide

The scientific method is a process scientists use to investigate questions using observations, fair tests, and evidence. It helps you explain what happened and how you know. You may repeat steps or change your experiment as you learn.

  1. STEP 1

    Make an observation

    Notice something interesting about the world.

    Example: “Some paper towels seem to soak up spills better than others.”

  2. STEP 2

    Ask a testable question

    Ask a question you can answer by collecting measurements or observations. Make the question specific.

    Example: “Which of three paper towel types absorbs the most water when the pieces are the same size?”

    “Which paper towel is best?” is too vague until you decide what “best” means. Best at soaking up water? Strongest when wet? Cheapest?

  3. STEP 3

    Do background research

    Learn about your topic before testing it. Use trustworthy books, classroom resources, and teacher-approved websites. Write down where your information came from.

    Example: “Research how paper fibers absorb and hold water.”

  4. STEP 4

    Form a hypothesis

    A hypothesis is a possible explanation that you can test. Use it to make a prediction about your experiment.

    Sentence frame“If I change _____, then I predict _____ will happen because _____.”

    Example: “If I test equal-sized pieces of three paper towel types, then I predict the thicker type will absorb more water because it may have more material available to hold water.”

    A hypothesis does not have to turn out to be correct. Testing it is how you learn.

  5. STEP 5

    Plan a fair test

    Decide what you will change, what you will measure, and what you will keep the same.

    Words to know

    Independent variable
    The one factor you intentionally change.
    Dependent variable
    The result you measure.
    Controlled variables (constants)
    Conditions you keep the same so the comparison is fair.
    Control group
    A baseline group used for comparison when appropriate. Not every experiment needs a separate untreated group.
    Trial
    One repetition of a test.

    Paper towel example:

    • Change: Paper towel type.
    • Measure: Water absorbed in milliliters or grams.
    • Keep the same: Sample area, soaking time, draining time, and measuring procedure.

    Before you start, plan repeated trials, list your materials, identify safety needs, and prepare a data table.

  6. STEP 6

    Conduct the experiment and collect data

    Follow your procedure carefully. Record measurements as you take them, including units. Write down observations and anything unexpected that happens.

    Do not change or remove results just because they do not match your prediction.

    Example: “Test several fresh pieces of each towel type and record how much water each one absorbs.”

  7. STEP 7

    Analyze the results

    Organize your measurements. Calculate averages when appropriate and create a graph.

    Finding an averageTo find an average, add the measurements together and divide by the number of measurements.
    Example: (12 + 15 + 13) ÷ 3 = 13.3 g

    Look for patterns and differences. Consider how much your repeated measurements vary. If trials of the same test are far apart, your results are less certain.

    📊 Bar graphCompare categories, such as paper towel types.
    📈 Line graphShow change over time.
    ⚬ ScatterplotExplore the relationship between two number variables when appropriate.

    Label the graph, both axes, and the units.

  8. STEP 8

    Draw a conclusion

    Answer your original question using your actual results. Include:

    • What you found.
    • Specific measurements that support your answer.
    • Whether the results supported your hypothesis.
    • Limitations or possible sources of error.
    • What you would improve or test next.

    Sentence frame“My results showed _____. The evidence was _____. This supported / did not support my hypothesis because _____. One limitation was _____. Next time, I would _____.”

    One experiment does not prove a claim forever. An unexpected result can still be useful science.

  9. STEP 9

    Communicate your findings

    Share your project through a report, display board, slideshow, or classroom presentation. Include:

    • Title and question
    • Background research and sources
    • Hypothesis
    • Variables
    • Materials and procedure
    • Data table and graph
    • Conclusion
    • Improvements or future questions

    Use your own words and explain what you actually did.

📌 Helpful reminders

🌋 Experiment or Demonstration?

A demonstration shows how something works. An experiment tests a question by changing something and measuring the result.

  • Demonstration: Making a baking soda and vinegar volcano erupt.
  • Experiment: Changing the amount of baking soda while keeping the vinegar amount the same, and measuring the gas produced with an appropriate, teacher-approved setup.

Never seal a gas-producing reaction inside a closed bottle or rigid container.

🏆 What Makes a Strong Science Project?

  • A clear, testable question
  • One main factor changed at a time
  • Careful measurements with units
  • Fair comparisons
  • Repeated trials or multiple samples
  • Honest records
  • A conclusion supported by data

🚧 Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Changing several things at once
  • Testing only once
  • Forgetting units
  • Measuring different groups in different ways
  • Waiting until the last minute for a growing project
  • Writing a conclusion before collecting results
  • Copying someone else's results
  • Assuming an unsupported hypothesis means the project failed

✅ Before You Begin

  • My teacher approved my project.
  • I have the supplies and enough time.
  • I know what I will change and measure.
  • I have planned repeated trials or multiple samples.
  • I have a blank data table.
  • I understand the safety directions.

🥽 Science Project Safety

Every project page has its own safety notes. These rules apply to every project:

  • Get teacher approval before beginning.
  • Use adult help for cutting tools, heated water, and electrical construction.
  • Electrical projects must use approved low-voltage batteries or educational kits.
  • Never connect experiment wires, batteries, or homemade circuits to household outlets, and never short-circuit a battery on purpose.
  • Do not taste experiment materials.
  • Do not grow unknown bacteria or mold.
  • Do not use flames, hazardous chemicals, or pressurized sealed containers.
  • Do not do experiments that harm people or animals.
