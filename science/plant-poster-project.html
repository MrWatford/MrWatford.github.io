🌱 About this project

Plants are all around us, but each kind has features that make it special. For this project, you will choose one specific plant or tree, research it, and create an organized poster board. Your poster will help others identify your plant, understand where it grows, and learn why it matters.

  • 🌳Trees are plantsYou may choose a tree or another kind of plant, such as a flower, a vegetable plant, a fern, or a houseplant.
  • 🙋Individual projectYou will research, write, and design your poster on your own.
  • 🔍One specific plantYou are researching one kind of plant, not plants in general.
  • 📚Research, not an experimentYou do not need to grow a plant or do an experiment.
  • 🍂Real specimens are optionalClear drawings and photographs can meet every requirement. Real leaves and bark rubbings are extras.
  • ⭐Quality over costAccurate research and thoughtful work matter more than expensive supplies.

Due date: To be announced by Mr. Watford.

🌻 Choose a specific plant or tree

Choose a plant or tree that interests you, then have your choice approved by Mr. Watford. Select a specific kind so your facts, pictures, and scientific name all describe the same plant.

  • Instead of “an oak,” choose southern live oak.
  • Instead of “a maple,” choose Japanese maple.
  • Instead of “a flower,” choose common sunflower.

🏷️ Check the scientific name

One common name can describe more than one species. For example, many different trees are called “oak.” Confirm your plant’s scientific name with a reliable source before you start taking notes.

🛒 Research only

These are research options, not instructions to buy, plant, or collect them. Mushrooms and other fungi are not plants, so they are not choices for this project.

📋 What the completed poster must include

  1. Large common-name title.
  2. Correct scientific name beneath or near the title.
  3. One large photograph or student-created illustration showing the whole plant.
  4. A clearly labeled Plant Facts section.
  5. Three short research paragraphs in your own words.
  6. Three labeled close-up visuals showing identifying features.
  7. A small map showing the plant’s native range.
  8. Three additional interesting facts (Did You Know?).
  9. At least two reliable research sources.
  10. Your name and class on the back.

📐 Board size

Use a standard poster board, about 22 × 28 inches, turned tall (vertical) or wide (horizontal). Check with Mr. Watford before using a much different size.

✏️ Typed or handwritten

Typed text or neat handwriting is fine. Either way, it must be easy to read.

🍂 Real specimens?

Real leaves, bark rubbings, and lightweight natural materials are optional. They may take the place of a close-up visual, but they must be labeled and correctly identified.

🔎 Research guide: Plant Facts

Find each item below and report it in your Plant Facts section. Short labeled bullets or small fact cards work better than long paragraphs. Write down which source each fact came from. The research organizer has a space for every topic.

A Common and scientific names

Find the common name and the scientific name.

A scientific name helps people identify exactly which plant they mean, even when common names differ.

  • Capitalize the genus (first word).
  • Lowercase the species name (second word).
  • Typed: italicize both words.
  • Handwritten: underline each word.

Southern magnolia — Magnolia grandiflora
Southern magnolia — Magnolia grandiflora

Copy the spelling and capitals from a reliable source, not from a poster you have seen.

B Plant type

Describe its growth form: tree, shrub, vine, or herbaceous plant.

Herbaceous plants have stems that are not persistently woody like a tree trunk.

Also tell its plant group: flowering plant, conifer, fern, or another group.

These labels answer different questions. A southern magnolia is both a tree and a flowering plant. A bald cypress is both a tree and a conifer.

C Typical size and growth

  • Typical mature height
  • Typical width or spread, if your source gives it
  • Whether it grows quickly or slowly, if your source says

Use units and a reasonable range, like “40–80 feet tall.” A record-breaking giant is a fun fact, not the typical size. If you measured a real plant, report that separately from the typical size of the species.

D Leaves

Describe the leaves’ shape, color, and edges (smooth, toothed, or lobed). Add the arrangement on the stem and whether they are simple or compound, if you can tell.

Simple leaf
One undivided blade, which may still have lobes.
Compound leaf
A leaf divided into separate leaflets.

Conifer? Describe its needles or scales. Fern? Describe its fronds.

E Stem, trunk, or bark

Tree or woody shrub: Describe the bark’s color and texture, and the trunk or branching pattern.

Nonwoody plant: Describe its stem or other supporting structure instead.

Nonwoody plants do not need a bark description or a bark rubbing.

F Flowers, fruits, cones, seeds, or spores

Explain how your plant makes new plants. Research only the structures your plant actually has:

  • Flowers
  • Fruits and seeds
  • Cones and seeds (conifers)
  • Spores (ferns and other spore-producing plants)

Not every plant makes flowers or fruits. Describe the structures your plant actually has. A cone is not a flower, and a spore is not a seed.

G Native range and habitat

Where does it grow naturally? In what habitats is it commonly found? Do people also grow it in other places?

Native range
Places where the species occurs naturally, rather than places where people introduced it.
Habitat
The kind of environment where it lives, like a wetland, forest, or sandy coast.

Keep these separate. Japanese maple is native to East Asia. People plant it in yards in Alabama, but that does not make Alabama part of its native range.

H Growing needs

In simple words, describe what it needs:

  • Sunlight (full sun, part shade, shade)
  • Water or moisture
  • Soil and drainage
  • General climate

Don’t make up an exact watering schedule. Water needs change with the pot, season, and place.

I Seasonal changes or life cycle

Describe whichever applies: evergreen or deciduous, flowering or fruiting season, and annual, biennial, or perennial.

Evergreen
Keeps foliage throughout the year, while replacing individual leaves over time.
Deciduous
Loses its leaves seasonally.
Annual
Completes its life cycle in one growing season.
Biennial
Usually completes its life cycle over two growing seasons.
Perennial
Lives for more than two years.

Evergreen does not mean a plant never sheds leaves. It drops old leaves a few at a time, so it is never bare.

J Importance to nature and people

Find at least:

  • One role in nature, such as food, shelter, or resources for pollinators or other animals.
  • One human use or reason people value it, if it has one.

Don’t assume a plant is edible or medicinal. Report uses as facts only; never taste a plant or use one to treat an illness.

If your plant is invasive somewhere, explain that accurately and name the region. Non-native just means it did not originally grow in an area. Invasive means a non-native plant spreads and causes harm in a particular region. They are not the same thing.

✍️ Three-paragraph research summary

Write three short paragraphs of about 3–5 sentences each. Explain your research in your own words.

About the sentence starters: They help you get going. They are not finished paragraphs to copy. Change the wording and make it sound like you!

Paragraph 1

Meet my plant

  • Common and scientific names
  • Plant type
  • Where it naturally grows
  • Why you chose it

Sentence starter“The plant I researched is _____. Its scientific name is _____. It is a type of _____. I chose it because _____.”

Paragraph 2

Identify my plant

  • Overall appearance and size
  • Leaves
  • Stem or bark
  • Flowers, fruits, cones, or other structures that apply

Sentence starter“You can recognize this plant by _____. Its leaves _____. Another identifying feature is _____.”

Paragraph 3

Explain its importance

  • Growing needs or habitat
  • A role in nature
  • A human use, if it has one
  • Something interesting you learned

Sentence starter“This plant grows well in _____. It is important in nature because _____. One thing I learned was _____.”

🔬 Three close-up visuals

Besides your whole-plant picture, include three different labeled close-ups of features that help someone identify your plant. Choose the ones that fit your plant:

  • Leaf, needle, or frond
  • Bark or stem
  • Flower
  • Fruit
  • Seed or cone
  • Another identifying structure

Each visual needs

  1. A clear label.
  2. A short caption telling the reader what to notice.

“Leaf: Notice the lobed edges.”
“Bark: The surface has deep grooves.”
“Seed pod: Seeds develop inside this structure.”

These show the caption format. They are not facts about every plant. Write captions that are true for yours.

Your visuals can be

  • Your own drawings
  • Your own photographs
  • Printed photographs (with a credit)
  • Safe, dried specimens
  • Leaf or bark rubbings

Show a mix of features. Three pictures of nearly the same leaf only count as one.

🍁 Add a Nature Sample—If You Can Do So Safely

This is optional. You can fully complete the project without collecting anything. Drawings and photographs work great.

Pressed leaf

  1. Have an adult help identify a safe leaf.
  2. Collect only where permitted. A recently fallen leaf is best.
  3. Place it between absorbent paper, like paper towels or newspaper.
  4. Put it beneath a heavy book (ask permission first).
  5. Let it dry for several days or longer. Check it and replace damp paper as needed.
  6. Attach the dry leaf securely and label it.

Thick or waxy leaves may not press well. A photograph is a good alternative.

Leaf rubbing

  1. Place a safe leaf beneath thin paper.
  2. Rub gently with the side of a crayon.
  3. Label the outline and the visible veins.

Bark rubbing

  1. Ask permission and have an adult help choose a safe, easy-to-reach tree.
  2. Hold paper flat against the trunk.
  3. Rub gently with the side of a crayon.
  4. Remove the paper without marking or damaging the tree.
  5. Label it with the tree’s name.

Never peel bark or attach paper with nails or staples.

⚠️ Collection rules

  • Do not touch or collect unidentified, irritating, or poisonous plants.
  • Do not taste leaves, berries, seeds, or sap.
  • Do not climb trees or cut branches for this project.
  • Do not collect from protected areas or private property without permission.
  • Avoid thorns, insects, and moldy materials.
  • Use photographs when identification or collection is uncertain.
  • Follow classroom allergy rules before bringing nuts, pollen-bearing materials, or other specimens.

Keep it light: no large branches or heavy objects on your poster.

🗺️ Native-range map

Include one small, clearly labeled map. You may:

  • Shade a blank map using information from a reliable source, or
  • Use a published range map and credit it.

Your map needs:

  • A map title
  • A simple key explaining the shading
  • A source

Mr. WatfordA native-range map shows where the species naturally occurs. It does not mean the plant grows in every location inside the shaded area.

Show only the native range, or use a second color with its own key entry for places where people plant it. Never shade a map just to decorate it. Every shaded area must come from your source.

💡 Did You Know?

Add a section on your board titled Did You Know? with three facts that add something beyond your Plant Facts. Questions you could investigate:

  • How did the plant get its name?
  • Does it have an unusual way of spreading seeds?
  • Does a particular animal rely on it?
  • Does it have a special cultural or historical use?
  • Does it have an unusual seasonal feature?

Mr. WatfordChoose facts you understand and can explain. Check surprising claims with a reliable source.

🧭 Step-by-step work plan

  1. Choose your plant. Get approval for one specific plant or tree.
  2. Find sources. Start with at least two reliable references.
  3. Take notes. Complete the research organizer and record your sources.
  4. Plan your pictures. Choose a whole-plant image and three identifying close-ups.
  5. Make your map. Show the native range clearly.
  6. Write your paragraphs. Explain the research in your own words.
  7. Sketch the board. Plan all sections before attaching anything.
  8. Prepare materials. Print, draw, label, and dry any optional specimens.
  9. Arrange and attach. Keep the layout readable and secure.
  10. Check everything. Proofread, verify facts, add sources, and put your name and class on the back.

Mr. WatfordStart early if you want to press a leaf. Do not wait until the final night.

🎨 What the poster should look like

Great plant and tree posters often have:

  • Large common-name headings
  • Scientific names near the titles
  • Research mounted on contrasting paper
  • A whole-tree or whole-plant picture
  • Labeled leaves and other identifying features
  • Bark rubbings (for trees)
  • A small range map
  • Coordinated borders and natural colors

Green, brown, cream, and other readable color combinations work well, but you may choose your own theme. You do not have to copy any example exactly.

🔠 Make it readable

  • Make the title readable from several feet away.
  • Keep body text readable at normal viewing distance.
  • Use clear section headings.

🗂️ Keep it organized

  • Mount text on contrasting paper.
  • Leave space between sections.
  • Keep decorations from covering facts.
  • Use captions to explain photographs and specimens.

📚 Reliable sources and original work

Use at least two reliable sources. Read the source itself. A search-results snippet or an AI response is not a substitute for a research source.

List each source in this format:

Organization or author — Page or book title — Website address or page numbers — Date accessed

Website[Organization] — [Page title] — [Website address] — Accessed [month, day, year]

Book[Author or publisher] — [Book title] — Pages [__–__] — Read [month, day, year]

ImageImage: [what it shows] — [Organization or photographer] — [Website address]

Your ownImage: [what it shows] — Student-created drawing (or photograph)

Credit every borrowed picture and map. Label your own drawings and photographs as student-created.

Your own work

  • Do not copy a website, another student’s work, or an AI-generated report.
  • Be able to explain everything on your board.
  • Adults may help with supplies and safe tool use, but the research, writing, and design should be your work.
