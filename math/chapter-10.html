📐 Angles

Angles are measured in degrees (°). An acute angle is less than 90°, a right angle is exactly 90°, an obtuse angle is between 90° and 180°, and a straight angle is 180°.

Complementary

Two angles that add to 90°.

Supplementary

Two angles that add to 180°.

Vertical angles

Opposite angles formed by two crossing lines. They are always equal.

Parallel lines + a transversal

Corresponding angles are equal, and alternate interior angles are equal.

✏️ Worked examples

Example 1: Complement

Find the complement of a 35° angle.

90 − 35.

Answer: 55°

Example 2: Supplement

Find the supplement of a 112° angle.

180 − 112.

Answer: 68°

Example 3: Algebra

Two supplementary angles measure x° and (2x + 30)°. Find x.

x + 2x + 30 = 180, so 3x = 150.

Answer: x = 50

⬡ Polygons

A polygon is a closed figure made of straight sides. It's named by its number of sides: triangle (3), quadrilateral (4), pentagon (5), hexagon (6), octagon (8), decagon (10).

Angle sum

The interior angles of a polygon with n sides add up to (n − 2) × 180°.
Triangle: 180°. Quadrilateral: 360°. Pentagon: 540°.

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal. Each angle = angle sum ÷ n.

✏️ Worked examples

Example 1: Missing triangle angle

A triangle has angles of 48° and 67°. Find the third.

180 − 48 − 67.

Answer: 65°

Example 2: Angle sum

Find the sum of the interior angles of a hexagon.

(6 − 2) × 180 = 4 × 180.

Answer: 720°

Example 3: Regular polygon

Find each angle of a regular octagon.

Sum: 6 × 180 = 1,080. Divide by 8.

Answer: 135°

📏 The Pythagorean Theorem

In a right triangle, the two shorter sides are the legs (a and b), and the longest side, across from the right angle, is the hypotenuse (c).

a2 + b2 = c2

The sum of the squares of the legs equals the square of the hypotenuse.

  • Find the hypotenuse: add the squares, then take the square root.
  • Find a leg: subtract the squares, then take the square root.
  • Is it a right triangle? Check whether a2 + b2 = c2 with c as the longest side.

Common whole-number sets: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25, and their multiples.

✏️ Worked examples

Example 1: Find the hypotenuse

Legs 6 and 8

36 + 64 = 100, and √100 = 10.

Answer: 10

Example 2: Find a leg

Hypotenuse 13, one leg 5

169 − 25 = 144, and √144 = 12.

Answer: 12

Example 3: Real life

A 10-foot ladder leans on a wall with its base 6 feet out. How high does it reach?

100 − 36 = 64, and √64 = 8.

Answer: 8 feet

🗺️ Coordinate Geometry

Distance

Draw a right triangle between the points and use the Pythagorean theorem:
d = √((x2 − x1)2 + (y2 − y1)2)

Midpoint

Average the x's and average the y's:
((x1 + x2)/2, (y1 + y2)/2)

If two points are on the same horizontal or vertical line, the distance is just the difference of the coordinates that change.

✏️ Worked examples

Example 1: Distance

(1, 2) and (4, 6)

Δx = 3, Δy = 4. √(9 + 16) = √25.

Answer: 5

Example 2: Midpoint

(2, 8) and (6, −4)

x: (2 + 6)/2 = 4. y: (8 + (−4))/2 = 2.

Answer: (4, 2)

Example 3: Same line

(−3, 5) and (7, 5)

Same y, so just 7 − (−3).

Answer: 10

🔺 Congruence and Similarity

Congruent (≅)

Same shape and same size. Matching sides and matching angles are equal.

Similar (~)

Same shape, possibly a different size. Matching angles are equal, and matching sides are proportional.

To find a missing side of similar figures, set up a proportion with matching sides. The ratio of matching sides is the scale factor.

Mr. WatfordShadow problems use similar triangles: a person and a tree with their shadows make two triangles with the same angles.

✏️ Worked examples

Example 1: Congruent sides

△ABC ≅ △DEF and AB = 9 cm. Find DE.

AB matches DE, and congruent sides are equal.

Answer: 9 cm

Example 2: Missing side

Similar triangles: sides 4 and 6 match sides 10 and x.

4/10 = 6/x, so 4x = 60.

Answer: x = 15

Example 3: Shadows

A 6-foot man casts a 4-foot shadow. A tree casts a 20-foot shadow. How tall is the tree?

6/4 = t/20, so 4t = 120.

Answer: 30 feet

🪞 Translations and Reflections

A transformation moves a figure to a new position. The new figure is called the image, and we label its points with a prime mark: A becomes A′.

Translation (slide)

Every point moves the same distance and direction.
Right/left changes x. Up/down changes y.
(x, y) → (x + a, y + b)

Reflection (flip)

Over the x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y)
Over the y-axis: (x, y) → (−x, y)

Translations and reflections keep the same size and shape, so the image is congruent to the original.

✏️ Worked examples

Example 1: Translation

Move (2, −3) 4 units left and 5 units up.

x: 2 − 4 = −2. y: −3 + 5 = 2.

Answer: (−2, 2)

Example 2: Reflect over x-axis

Reflect (5, 4) over the x-axis.

Keep x, change the sign of y.

Answer: (5, −4)

Example 3: Reflect over y-axis

Reflect (−3, 7) over the y-axis.

Change the sign of x, keep y.

Answer: (3, 7)

🔄 Rotations and Dilations

Rotation (turn) about the origin

90° counterclockwise: (x, y) → (−y, x)
180°: (x, y) → (−x, −y)
90° clockwise: (x, y) → (y, −x)

Dilation (resize) from the origin

Multiply both coordinates by the scale factor k:
(x, y) → (kx, ky)
k > 1 enlarges; 0 < k < 1 shrinks.

Rotations keep size and shape (congruent). Dilations keep shape but change size (similar).

✏️ Worked examples

Example 1: Rotate 90° counterclockwise

(3, 1)

(x, y) → (−y, x), so (−1, 3).

Answer: (−1, 3)

Example 2: Rotate 180°

(−4, 2)

Change both signs.

Answer: (4, −2)

Example 3: Dilate

(6, −2) with scale factor 1/2

Multiply each coordinate by 1/2.

Answer: (3, −1)
